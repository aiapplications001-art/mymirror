1. The Biochemistry of Chemical Peeling: AHA vs BHA Acid Science

Chemical peels utilize organic acids to accelerate the natural desquamation (shedding) of dead skin cells. The most effective at-home peeling solutions combine both Alpha Hydroxy Acids (AHAs) and Beta Hydroxy Acids (BHAs) to target different layers of the skin simultaneously.

AHAs (Glycolic, Lactic, Tartaric, Citric, Mandelic) are water-soluble acids derived from sugary fruits. They operate primarily on the surface of the stratum corneum by dissolving the desmosome bonds holding dead, dull skin cells together. This surface exfoliation reveals brighter skin, fades hyperpigmentation, and smooths rough texture.

BHA (Salicylic Acid) is an oil-soluble acid. Because of its lipophilic nature, BHA can bypass the skin's surface lipid barrier and penetrate deep inside the sebaceous follicles (pores). Once inside, it dissolves sebum, clearing out blackheads, whiteheads, and congestions that lead to acne.

Why Multi-Acid Blends Attack Both Surface Texture AND Pore Congestion

A multi-acid blend—like a 30% AHA + 2% BHA solution—delivers a comprehensive resurfacing treatment. While the AHAs sweep away the superficial layers of dead skin to improve radiance and texture, the BHA works concurrently to deep-clean the pores and reduce inflammation. This dual-action approach makes these peels highly effective for treating both acne and the post-acne marks left behind.

2. Free Acid Value & pH: Why Label Percentage Doesn't Tell the Full Story

When you purchase an at-home chemical peel, the percentage written on the bottle (e.g., 30% AHA) is not the most important factor. The true measure of a peel's potency is its Free Acid Value (FAV), which is determined by the formula's pH level.

The pH scale measures how acidic or alkaline a substance is. For chemical exfoliants to be active, they must be formulated at an acidic pH. If a product's pH is too high (alkaline), the acids are neutralized into salts, rendering them ineffective for exfoliation.

⚠️ The Importance of pH in Chemical Peels

Consider this: A 30% glycolic acid formula at a pH of 3.6 delivers a significantly higher amount of active, available acid (Free Acid Value) than a 30% formula buffered to a pH of 4.5.

How buffering agents neutralize acid potency: Formulators often add buffers (like sodium hydroxide) to raise the pH, making the peel less irritating but also less potent. Why The Ordinary's peeling solution is so powerful is precisely because it is formulated at a very low pH of ~3.6, meaning a vast majority of its 30% AHA concentration is 'free' and active to rapidly exfoliate your skin.

5. The 10-Minute At-Home Peel Protocol (Step-by-Step)

High-strength peeling solutions are not daily serums; they are flash treatments that require strict adherence to protocol to prevent chemical burns.

1
Cleanse & Dry Completely
Wash your face with a gentle cleanser. Pat dry thoroughly. Do not apply peels to wet skin—water can drive the acids deeper and faster, increasing the risk of burns.
2
Apply & Start Timer
Apply an even layer using your fingertips, avoiding the eye contour. Set a timer for 5 to 10 minutes. If it's your first time, start with 5 minutes.
3
Rinse & Repair
Rinse thoroughly with lukewarm water. Follow immediately with a hydrating Hyaluronic Acid serum and a thick Ceramide cream. Skip all other actives.

6. ⚠️ Chemical Peel Safety: When NOT to Use At-Home Peels

While at-home peels are safer than professional in-office treatments, they are still potent acids capable of causing severe damage if misused.

🚨 DO NOT USE AT-HOME PEELS IF:

  • You have an active sunburn: Exfoliating sun-damaged skin will cause intense pain and scarring.
  • You have open wounds or picked pimples: The acid will enter the dermis directly, causing severe chemical burns and risking infection.
  • It's your retinoid night: Never layer strong peels with Tretinoin, Adapalene, or Retinol.
  • You have a compromised barrier: If your skin is currently stinging, red, tight, or flaking, your barrier is broken. Adding acids will worsen the condition.
  • You have active Rosacea: High-strength acids can trigger severe rosacea flare-ups.
  • During Pregnancy: Consult your OB-GYN; high doses of Salicylic Acid (BHA) are generally contraindicated during pregnancy.

8. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Expert answers regarding AHA BHA peeling solutions and at-home safety:

For most skin types, no more than once a week. If you have sensitive skin, start with once every two weeks or reduce the contact time to 5 minutes instead of the full 10 minutes.
Do not use strong chemical peels on the same night as retinoids (retinol, tretinoin, adapalene). In fact, dermatologists recommend skipping retinoids 24-48 hours before and after using a strong at-home peel to prevent barrier damage.
Mild tingling is normal as the acids lower the pH of your skin and dissolve dead cells. However, intense burning, stinging, or redness indicates your skin barrier is compromised, and you should rinse the peel off immediately.
AHAs (Alpha Hydroxy Acids) are water-soluble and exfoliate the surface of the skin, improving texture and hyperpigmentation. BHAs (Beta Hydroxy Acids, like Salicylic Acid) are oil-soluble and penetrate deeply into pores to clear sebum and blackheads.
Yes, absolutely. After rinsing off the peel and patting your skin dry, apply a soothing, barrier-repairing moisturizer containing ceramides or hyaluronic acid to restore hydration. Avoid other active ingredients like Vitamin C or benzoyl peroxide.

Quick Derm Summary

  • pH Matters: Look for formulas with a pH between 3.5 and 4.0.
  • 10-Minute Max: Never leave strong peels on longer than 10 minutes.
  • Retinoid Break: Stop retinoids 48hrs before and after peeling.
  • Hydration: Always follow with ceramides and hyaluronic acid.
