Master Glossary: Decoding Medical Shorthand

Indian doctors rely on classic Latin medical abbreviations alongside a localized 3-digit zero system (1-0-1). Here is the comprehensive breakdown:

1. Frequency Abbreviations

Code Latin Meaning English Meaning Clinical Guidance
OD Omne in Die Once Daily Apply or take once per 24 hours (usually specified AM or PM).
BD / BID Bis in Die Twice Daily Apply or take every 12 hours (morning and night).
TDS / TID Ter in Die Three Times Daily Take every 8 hours. Commonly used for oral anti-inflammatory enzymes.
HS / QHS Hora Somni At Bedtime (Night) Crucial for retinoids (Adapalene, Tretinoin) which degrade in sunlight.
QOD Quaque Altera Die Every Other Day Prescribed during weeks 1–2 of retinoid induction to prevent peeling.
SOS / PRN Si Opus Sit As Needed / Emergency Used for hydrocolloid pimple patches or acute spot treatments.

2. The Indian "Zero" Dosage Notation (1-0-1)

In Indian hospital OPDs and private clinics, dermatologists write a 3-digit pattern representing Morning – Afternoon – Night:

  • 1-0-0: Morning only (e.g., Clindamycin gel or oral Vitamin C).
  • 0-0-1: Night only (e.g., Adapalene or Tretinoin gel).
  • 1-0-1: Morning and Night (e.g., Oral Doxycycline 100mg or Salicylic acid wash).
  • 0-1-0: Afternoon only (e.g., specific post-lunch oral zinc supplements).

3. Application Technique & Area Codes

Code Full Form How to Execute
AAA Apply to Affected Area Dab active gel strictly on active inflammatory pimples, not on surrounding clear skin.
SCT Short Contact Therapy Apply gel to dry skin, leave on for 15–30 minutes, then wash off thoroughly with water.
Whole Face Field Application Spread a tiny pea-sized drop across the whole face to prevent future microcomedones.
PC / AC Post / Ante Cibum PC = After Food; AC = Before Food (essential for oral Doxycycline to prevent stomach upset).

The 5 Core Pillars of an Indian Acne Prescription

Dermatologists rarely rely on a single "magic cream." Because acne involves sebum overproduction, hyperkeratinization, bacterial proliferation, and inflammation, an effective prescription coordinates 5 complementary pillars:

💡

Why Multimodal Therapy is Required

Monotherapy (using only 1 active) often leads to treatment failure or bacterial resistance. Combining a morning antibacterial with an evening keratolytic retinoid yields a 3x higher clinical clearing rate.

Pillar 1: The Exfoliating or Medicated Cleanser

Primes the stratum corneum by clearing sebum plugs. Common choices include Salicylic Acid 2% Washes (Saslic DS, Cipla Saliwash) for comedonal acne, or Benzoyl Peroxide 4-5% Washes (Acnestar, Perobar) for papulopustular acne.

Pillar 2: Morning (AM) Anti-Inflammatory / Antimicrobial Active

Suppresses daytime bacterial activity and redness. Top choices:

  • Clindamycin 1% Gel (Clindac-A, Erytop): Rapidly calms active red pus bumps.
  • Azelaic Acid 15% Gel (Aziderm, Picspot): Dual-action powerhouse that targets acne and fades Post-Inflammatory Hyperpigmentation (PIH).
  • Niacinamide 4-5% Gel: Strengthens skin barrier lipids while regulating sebum.

Pillar 3: Broad-Spectrum Non-Comedogenic Sunscreen

Prescription active ingredients increase photosensitivity. Dermatologists prescribe silicone-gel or fluid sunscreens (Episoft AC, UV Doux, Acne-UV Gel) that provide high UVA/UVB protection without clogging pores on Indian skin (Fitzpatrick IV–VI).

Pillar 4: Nighttime (PM) Retinoid or Keratolytic

The cornerstone of long-term acne resolution. Normalizes skin cell shedding and clears microcomedones before they turn into pimples:

  • Adapalene 0.1% Gel (Adaferin, Deriva MS): First-line retinoid; selective RAR-beta/gamma receptor targeting with minimal irritation.
  • Tretinoin 0.025% Cream (Retino-A): Potent classic retinoid; requires careful buffering.
  • Glycolic Acid 6% Cream (Glyco-6): AHA alternative for patients intolerant to retinoids.

Pillar 5: Systemic Oral Medications (Grade 3 & 4 Acne)

For moderate-to-severe nodulocystic acne, oral intervention is necessary:

  • Oral Doxycycline 100mg: Taken 1-0-1 PC for 2 to 4 weeks to halt acute systemic inflammation.
  • Oral Isotretinoin 10mg / 20mg (Tretiva, Sotret): Low-dose retinoid capsule that shrinks sebaceous glands at the root.

Application Protocols: Sandwich Method & Short Contact Therapy

Most prescription acne gels fail not because the medicine is wrong, but because of incorrect application technique. Here are the two gold-standard clinical protocols Indian dermatologists recommend to avoid chemical burns and peeling:

1. The Sandwich Method (For Retinoid Beginners)

Designed to buffer potent active penetration on sensitive or dry Indian skin:

  1. Wash face with a gentle cleanser and pat 100% dry.
  2. Apply a thin layer of light ceramide moisturizer. Wait 5 minutes.
  3. Dot a pea-sized amount of Adapalene or Tretinoin gel evenly across the face.
  4. Wait another 5 minutes, then seal with a second light layer of moisturizer.

2. Short Contact Therapy (SCT for High-Strength Actives)

Ideal for patients who experience stinging or burning with Benzoyl Peroxide or high-strength Azelaic Acid:

⏱️

SCT Step-by-Step

Apply the active gel to clean, dry skin. Leave it on for exactly 15 to 30 minutes. Wash off thoroughly with cool water, pat dry, and apply moisturizer. You gain 85%+ of the clinical therapeutic benefit while eliminating overnight barrier destruction.

🚨 STEROID RED FLAGS: The Epidemic of TSDF in India

One of the most dangerous occurrences in Indian skincare is Topical Steroid Damaged Face (TSDF). Patients often seek quick remedies from OTC pharmacies and are handed potent "triple-combination" fairness or anti-fungal creams.

⚠️

NEVER Apply These Steroid Creams to Facial Acne!

The following popular OTC creams contain ultra-potent corticosteroids (Clobetasol, Betamethasone). While they temporarily hide redness for 48 hours, they destroy skin immunity, cause permanent skin thinning (atrophy), telangiectasia (visible blood vessels), and trigger severe steroid-rebound acne & rosacea:

Frequently Asked Questions

What does '1-0-1' or '0-0-1' mean on an Indian prescription?
The zero notation system indicates dosing timing: Morning – Afternoon – Night. '1-0-1' means take or apply one dose in the morning and one dose at night (BD/twice daily). '0-0-1' means take or apply one dose only at night before bed (HS/QHS).
What does 'SCT' stand for on an acne gel prescription?
SCT stands for Short Contact Therapy. It is a dermatological technique where potent active ingredients (like Benzoyl Peroxide or Adapalene) are applied to clean skin, left on for a short duration (usually 15 to 30 minutes), and then washed off completely to minimize irritation while preserving efficacy.
What does 'AAA' mean on my topical gel tube?
AAA stands for 'Apply to Affected Area'. It instructs you to apply the medication only on active pimples or affected skin regions rather than smearing it across unaffected parts of your face.
Why did my dermatologist prescribe both a Morning (AM) and Night (PM) active?
Acne has multiple causes: excess oil, bacterial colonization (C. acnes), rapid skin cell buildup, and inflammation. Dermatologists pair daytime anti-inflammatory/antibacterial treatments (like Clindamycin or Azelaic Acid) with nighttime retinoids (like Adapalene or Tretinoin) to attack acne from complementary pathways without compounding skin irritation.
How can I spot a dangerous steroid cream on an OTC prescription or pharmacy recommendation?
Check the active ingredients for words ending in '-sone', '-sol', or '-nide' (such as Clobetasol, Betamethasone, Mometasone). Creams like Betnovate-N, Panderm, Quadriderm, and Fourderm contain strong steroids that cause permanent skin thinning, steroid rosacea, and severe rebound acne if applied to the face.
Can I swap the brand name on my prescription for a generic equivalent at the chemist?
Yes, for Schedule H and OTC medications, Indian chemists can substitute branded generics (e.g., swapping Adaferin 0.1% Gel for Deriva MS 0.1% Gel) as long as the active salt (Adapalene 0.1%) and dosage form match exactly.
