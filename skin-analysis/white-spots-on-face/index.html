White Spots on Face — What Are They?

Pale or white patches on your face can look alarming, but most are harmless. This guide explains the 5 most common causes in plain English — so you know exactly what to do next.

Flat or raised?

That one detail changes everything. This guide helps you figure it out.

📋 Medical Disclaimer: This page is for educational guidance only. It does not diagnose any skin condition or replace a dermatologist's examination. If white patches are spreading rapidly, painful, or causing significant distress, please seek professional care promptly.

The short answer

What causes white spots on the face?

White or pale spots on the face are usually caused by one of five things: pityriasis alba (dry-skin patches, very common in kids), tinea versicolor (a mild fungal overgrowth), vitiligo (the immune system affecting pigment cells), post-inflammatory hypopigmentation (pale areas left behind after acne or a rash heals), or milia (tiny raised white bumps from trapped dead skin). Vitamin deficiency is a common assumption but is rarely the actual cause. A dermatologist can tell you which one it is — and each type needs a different response.

⚡ 10-Second Takeaway

The 2 questions that matter most

Before anything else, ask yourself these two things. They'll help you figure out what kind of white spot you're dealing with.

Is it flat or raised?

Flat = likely a pigment or dry-skin issue. Raised and firm = probably milia (a tiny trapped cyst).

Smooth or flaky?

Smooth edges = may be vitiligo or old hypopigmentation. Slight scale or flakiness = more likely pityriasis alba or fungal.

Spreading?

A patch that grows or multiplies over weeks deserves a dermatologist's eye — even if it's not painful.

Itchy or not?

Vitiligo is usually not itchy. Tinea versicolor can be mildly itchy when you sweat. Eczema-related spots often feel dry.

The 5 Most Common Causes — In Plain English

Each cause looks slightly different and needs a different response. Here's how to tell them apart without medical training.

🌤️
Most Common in Kids & Teens

Pityriasis Alba

These are faint, dry, slightly powdery pale patches — most often on the cheeks. They're linked to mild eczema-prone or dry skin and get more visible after the surrounding skin tans in summer.

Looks like: Hazy borders, slightly rough or flaky. Not stark white — more of a washed-out beige.
Itchy? Sometimes mildly.
What helps: Gentle moisturiser + daily SPF. Usually fades on its own.
🍄
Fungal (Yeast) Overgrowth

Tinea Versicolor

A harmless yeast that naturally lives on everyone's skin sometimes overgrows — especially in hot, humid conditions or with oily skin. It interferes with pigment and creates lighter (or sometimes darker) patches.

Looks like: Multiple small patches with very fine scale when scratched. Can appear on face, neck, chest.
Itchy? Can be mildly itchy when sweating.
What helps: Antifungal treatment — but only after a doctor confirms it.
🛡️
Autoimmune Condition

Vitiligo

The immune system mistakenly attacks the pigment cells (melanocytes). This causes clearly defined, smooth, chalk-white patches. It is not contagious, not caused by diet, and not dangerous to your health — but it deserves professional attention.

Looks like: Very sharp borders. Completely white — not pale or washed out. Sometimes symmetrical (both cheeks).
Itchy? Usually not.
What helps: Prescription topicals, phototherapy — requires a dermatologist.
🩹
After Acne or Rash

Post-Inflammatory Spots

When a pimple, eczema patch, or skin injury heals, the skin sometimes loses pigment temporarily — leaving a pale mark where the redness used to be. This is especially common in darker Indian skin tones.

Looks like: A flat, paler area in exactly the spot where a pimple or rash was. Fades over months.
Itchy? No.
What helps: Sun protection to prevent contrast. Avoid picking pimples.
⚪
Tiny Raised Bumps

Milia (Trapped Cysts)

Milia are small, firm, pearly-white bumps — not flat patches. Dead skin protein (keratin) gets trapped just under the surface. They are completely harmless but very common around the eyes, cheeks, and nose.

Looks like: Tiny hard white dot, like a grain of sand under the skin. Never has an opening.
Itchy? No.
What helps: Dermatologist extraction. Do not try to squeeze — no opening means nothing will come out.

The 5 Things Most People Get Wrong

These are the most common mistakes people make when they notice a white spot — and why each one can make things worse.

Most people react to white spots the same way — they grab whatever's in their cabinet and start applying. That instinct makes sense, but it almost always delays the right answer. The cause of a white spot determines the solution. Getting that backwards can irritate skin, mask the real issue, or make the spot more stubborn.

💡 Remember: White spots are often the opposite of dark spots — using dark spot treatments on them makes no difference.

Assuming it's a vitamin deficiency

This is the most common assumption in India — and it's usually wrong. White spots are caused by pigment changes, dry skin, fungal overgrowth, or milia — not vitamin B12 or calcium loss. Taking supplements without a diagnosis helps nothing and delays the right treatment.

Using brightening or fairness products

Brightening serums, vitamin C, and "dark spot" creams target excess pigment (hyperpigmentation — dark marks). White spots have the opposite problem — not enough pigment. Applying brightening products on them does nothing, and actives like vitamin C or acids can irritate sensitive skin around the spot.

Squeezing raised white bumps (milia)

Milia don't have an opening. Unlike pimples, nothing will come out when you squeeze — but you can break skin, cause inflammation, and leave a scar. A dermatologist removes them safely in seconds with a sterile needle.

Scrubbing or exfoliating the patch

It feels like the patch should scrub away. It won't — because the issue is in the pigment layer, not the surface. Harsh scrubbing irritates the skin barrier and can make the surrounding area red and inflamed, drawing more attention to the patch.

Waiting years before seeing a dermatologist (for vitiligo)

Vitiligo responds best to early treatment. The longer it goes untreated, the harder it becomes to restore pigment. If a white patch is very clearly defined, smooth, chalk-white, and spreading — don't wait. Early phototherapy or prescription topicals can significantly improve outcomes.

What You Can Safely Do Right Now

While you figure out what your white spot is — or while waiting to see a dermatologist — these steps are safe for all five types and won't make anything worse.

✅ The Safe Routine

  • Use SPF 50 every morning — without fail. Sun exposure makes pale patches more visible and can worsen all five types. This is the single most universal step.
  • Use a gentle, fragrance-free cleanser — something mild that doesn't strip the skin barrier.
  • Moisturise daily — especially for dry patches linked to pityriasis alba.
  • Don't switch products randomly — experimenting while you don't know the cause can irritate things further.
  • Take consistent photos — in the same lighting, every few weeks. This helps you and a dermatologist track whether the spot is growing or fading.

🩺 What to Tell Your Dermatologist

  • When did you first notice it — and has it changed since?
  • Is it one spot or several? Are they spreading?
  • Does it itch, hurt, feel numb, or feel dry?
  • Did a pimple, rash, eczema patch, or sunburn precede it?
  • Does it become more visible after sun exposure?
  • Any family history of vitiligo or autoimmune skin conditions?
  • Any new products, medicines, or procedures in the past few months?
  • Your photos (even a few phone pictures in good light help enormously).
⚠️ Go to a doctor sooner if the patch is spreading rapidly, hurts, bleeds, appears after a new medication, affects a large area of your face, or is significantly impacting your confidence or daily life.

How to Take a Clear Photo Before Your Scan or Appointment

A good photo makes all the difference — whether you're using MyMirror or showing a dermatologist.

  • 🌤️ Use natural daylight — sit near a window, face the light. Avoid flash (it washes out pale patches).
  • 📸 Take two photos — one close-up of the spot, one full face for context.
  • 💧 Avoid heavy moisturiser before — if the patch has texture or scale, that shine can hide it.
  • 🕐 Note the time of day — some patches look more obvious in certain lighting.
  • 🗓️ Repeat every 2-3 weeks — same light, same angle. Changes over time are your most useful data.
  • ✍️ Add a brief note — was the area itchy that day? Had you just come back from outdoors? These details help enormously.

Common Questions, Answered Simply

Are white spots on the face all the same thing? +

No — not at all. White or pale spots on the face can be caused by very different things. A dry-skin patch (pityriasis alba) looks similar to a fungal overgrowth (tinea versicolor), which looks similar to vitiligo — but each has different characteristics and needs a different response. The clue is usually in the texture (flaky vs smooth), the shape of the border (hazy vs sharp), and whether it's raised or flat.

Is it a vitamin deficiency? +

This is the most common assumption in India — and it's rarely correct. Most white spots on the face are caused by dry skin or mild eczema (pityriasis alba), fungal overgrowth (tinea versicolor), autoimmune pigment loss (vitiligo), post-inflammatory pale marks, or trapped keratin (milia). A dermatologist can tell you the actual cause, which may or may not involve nutrition.

Will sun exposure make white spots worse? +

Yes, in a visual sense. When the surrounding skin tans, pale patches become more noticeable by contrast. This is especially true for pityriasis alba — which is why it seems to "appear" in summer. It was likely always there, but becomes obvious once the rest of the face tans. Additionally, pale patches may be more sun-sensitive and burn more easily. Daily SPF 50 is the simplest way to protect the whole face.

Should I use vitamin C or brightening products on a white spot? +

Usually not. Brightening products are designed to reduce excess pigment (dark spots, hyperpigmentation). White spots have the opposite problem — areas with less pigment. Applying vitamin C, niacinamide, or kojic acid there is unlikely to help and may further irritate sensitive skin around the spot.

Is vitiligo contagious? +

No. Vitiligo is an autoimmune condition — the body's own immune system mistakenly attacks pigment cells. It has nothing to do with contact, touching, or any form of infection. It cannot be caught or passed to another person.

My child has white patches on their cheeks — should I worry? +

Most likely not — the most common cause in children and teenagers is pityriasis alba, a harmless condition linked to dry or eczema-prone skin. It usually resolves on its own with gentle moisturising and sun protection. That said, if the patch is spreading, clearly white (not just pale), or hasn't improved after 3 months, it's worth showing to a pediatrician or dermatologist for peace of mind.

Can MyMirror diagnose what type of white spot I have? +

No — MyMirror can help you map and track visible skin patterns, document how they change over time, and give you clearer language for a dermatologist conversation. But it cannot distinguish between vitiligo, tinea versicolor, pityriasis alba, or any other condition. That determination requires a clinical examination — sometimes with specialist tools like a Wood's lamp that makes certain conditions glow.

I see a tiny raised white bump near my eye — should I pop it? +

Please don't. Raised, firm, pearly white bumps near the eyes are almost certainly milia — tiny trapped keratin cysts that have no opening. Squeezing does nothing except break the skin and risk infection or scarring. A dermatologist can remove them safely in seconds with a sterile needle. No downtime, no scarring.
