Begin with a pattern, not a verdict

Observe what you see, what you feel, when it began and where it shows up. That is more useful than guessing a diagnosis from the mirror.

Quick answer

“Wrong” is a feeling. Your skin concern has clues.

A visible change can be caused by many things, and a photograph cannot confirm which one applies to you. It can, however, make the concern easier to describe. Is it raised or flat? Does it itch, sting or feel tender? Did it start after a routine change, or has it been returning in the same place? That observation does not replace medical advice; it gives you a calmer, more useful starting point.

Four clues before you name it

Try not to decide that every change must be “acne,” “an allergy,” or “sensitive skin” straight away. Start with these four clues. They work for a single new concern and for a routine that suddenly feels unreliable.

1

Appearance

Is it a bump, a flat mark, a dry patch, a cluster, a change in texture, or a change in how light reflects? Describe what you see without attaching a cause.

2

Sensation

Notice itch, heat, tightness, burning, tenderness or no sensation at all. A feeling can be an important reason to slow down rather than add another active product.

3

Timing

Ask whether it appeared quickly, has been repeating for weeks, or became noticeable after a new product, weather shift, haircut, medication or stress period.

4

Location

Is it near the hairline, centre face, cheeks, jaw, around the mouth, or everywhere? Location is a descriptive clue—not a shortcut to a diagnosis.

Seven visible patterns worth separating

Many concerns overlap. The aim is not to self-diagnose from this list; it is to stop treating every visible change as the same thing. A scan can help you keep track of the pattern you want to discuss or monitor.

Raised

Breakouts or bumps

Note whether they are isolated or clustered, tender or painless, and whether they leave a flat mark after they settle. Avoid picking, which can make marks more noticeable.

Flat colour

Dark marks or uneven tone

A flat area left after a blemish is different from an active bump. In deeper skin tones, lingering dark marks after acne can be especially noticeable and may last longer than the breakout itself.

Surface

Dryness, flaking or roughness

Look for a local patch versus a wider tight or rough feeling. Think back to cleansing, exfoliation, masks, retinoids, weather and hot-water exposure.

Shine

Oiliness or congestion

Shine around the centre face, visible pores and small bumps are separate observations. More cleansing is not automatically a better response if the skin also feels tight.

Colour shift

Redness, brownness or violet tone

Inflammation does not look identical on every complexion. On melanin-rich skin, a change may read as darker, brown or violet rather than bright red.

Reaction

Stinging or burning

A product that repeatedly stings, burns or tingles is not proof of “purging.” Treat it as a signal to pause and reassess, especially if you also see dryness or swelling.

Change

Something new or evolving

Photograph a change clearly and note when it began. A changing, bleeding, crusty or unusually itchy mole or lesion deserves clinical advice rather than cosmetic troubleshooting.

What changed recently?

Skin can react to a stack of small changes. The timeline is not there to blame your lifestyle; it is a way to avoid buying five new products while the original variable remains invisible.

  • Days: a new cleanser, mask, exfoliant, sunscreen, makeup, hair product, shaving step or a product used more often.
  • Weeks: seasonal heat or humidity, a move, travel, sleep disruption, stress, a workout routine or a change in laundry habits.
  • Months: a persistent pattern, a repeating cycle, a new medicine, or a mark that is not changing as expected.

A simple concern timeline

Write down the smallest useful sequence before you change anything else.

  1. First noticed: choose an approximate date, not a perfect memory.
  2. What changed nearby: list products, hair care, environment and habits without assuming one is guilty.
  3. What happened next: spread, settled, returned, stung, became tender, or left a mark.
  4. What you already tried: this prevents doubling up on strong steps just because the concern is frustrating.

Face-zone context: useful, but never a diagnosis shortcut

Where a concern appears can help you make a better note for yourself. It does not reliably reveal one medical cause. Use zones to check for contact, routine overlap and repetition—not to make a claim about hormones, diet or “toxins.”

Zones to inspect

  • Hairline and forehead: look at bangs, styling products, hats, helmets, fringe and sweat as possible points of contact.
  • Centre face: separate shine, visible pores and bumps; they may not need the same response.
  • Cheeks: notice old flat marks separately from active spots; check phone, pillowcase, makeup tools and mask friction.
  • Jaw and around the mouth: record tenderness, recurrence, shaving or oral-care contact rather than assuming a cause.

What helps more than a face-zone myth

  • Compare the two sides of your face in even daylight.
  • Notice if the pattern follows a product application path.
  • Record whether it is truly new, or just newly visible.
  • Check whether the skin feels different as well as looking different.
  • Use the same lighting for progress photos instead of judging from a different mirror each day.

A product-routine audit before you add more

When skin looks unsettled, the instinct is often to add a spot treatment, scrub, acid or mask. A calmer first move is to list what touches the area and identify where you may be stacking similar steps.

Check the basics

  • How often do you cleanse, and does your skin feel tight afterward?
  • Are you using more than one exfoliating, retinoid or acid-containing product?
  • Did you start a new sunscreen, makeup, hair product, fragrance or facial mask?
  • Are you applying strong products to already dry, peeling or irritated skin?
  • Are you changing several steps at once, making the pattern harder to read?

A safer way to test change

Dermatology guidance commonly recommends introducing products thoughtfully rather than all at once. If a product burns, stings or tingles, stop using it rather than pushing through for a promised result. For a prescription product, ask the prescriber what reactions are expected before stopping it. Patch-testing and professional advice can be important for persistent reactions.

For an otherwise mild concern, keep the routine simple long enough to observe it: gentle cleansing, moisturising if needed, and daily sun protection appropriate for you. This is not a treatment plan—it is a way to reduce noise while you decide on the next step.

Skin-tone-aware observations make the description more honest

A guide that only talks about obvious redness leaves many people without the vocabulary for what they are seeing. On brown and black skin, irritation or inflammation may look brown, violet, greyish, darker than surrounding skin, or show up first as heat, itch or texture. Acne can also leave dark marks that remain visible after the active spot has settled.

Describe the contrast

Instead of forcing a colour label, compare the area with nearby skin. Is it darker, lighter, more purple-toned, more textured or less even? Is the change flat or raised? This makes a scan result, personal note or dermatology conversation more precise.

Protect the skin you are trying to understand

Aggressive picking, scrubbing or rapidly layering actives can make irritation and visible marks worse. Sun exposure can also deepen some pigment changes. Consistent, suitable sun protection and a lower-irritation routine are practical foundations while you seek tailored advice.

Important boundary

When a scan is not the right next step

MyMirror should never delay care for a severe, rapidly changing or worrying symptom. A clear photo can be helpful information for a clinician, but it is not a substitute for an examination.

Seek prompt medical guidance for

  • Rapid swelling of the face, lips, tongue or throat, trouble breathing, or a severe reaction.
  • A painful, blistering, spreading or infected-looking rash; fever or feeling acutely unwell with a rash.
  • A mole or lesion that is changing, bleeding, crusty, persistently itchy or otherwise unusual.
  • Severe pain, sudden eye-area symptoms, or a skin concern that is affecting your wellbeing and not settling.
Prepare for your scan

Help your MyMirror scan see the clearest picture

Your MyMirror scan is most useful when the photo makes visible skin patterns easy to see. Choose even lighting, keep the image focused, and avoid filters that change tone or texture. You are not trying to create a perfect image—just a clear starting point for the concerns you want to understand.

Set up your scan photo

  • Use clean, even natural light where possible; avoid a strong overhead shadow.
  • Remove beauty filters and avoid a heavy makeup look that hides texture or tone.
  • Take a straight-on photo and use side angles if a concern is localised.
  • Keep hair away from the areas you want MyMirror to help you review.

Make each scan easier to compare

  • Use a similar time of day, distance and lighting for future scans.
  • Make a quick note of any important routine change before you begin.
  • Focus on the overall visible pattern rather than repeatedly zooming in on one spot.

Questions people ask when their skin suddenly feels different

Can a photo tell me exactly what is wrong with my skin?

No. A photo can document visible patterns and make a concern easier to describe, but it cannot confirm a diagnosis, rule out a condition or replace a clinician’s assessment.

Is it normal for my skin to change even if I have not changed products?

Skin can look different with weather, humidity, sleep disruption, stress, shaving, hair products, friction and other changes. A timeline helps you spot what changed nearby without assuming one cause.

Should I add a new active ingredient when I see texture or breakouts?

Adding several new actives can make a routine harder to read and may increase irritation. Start by identifying what you already use and seek personalised advice if the concern is persistent or uncomfortable.

Why do dark marks stay after a breakout has gone?

Flat pigment changes can remain after an active spot settles, particularly in skin of colour. They are visually different from active acne; sun protection and avoiding irritation are helpful basics while you seek tailored advice.

What if my skin does not look red?

Inflammation can appear as darker, brown, violet, greyish or uneven areas on deeper skin tones, and can also present with sensation or texture changes. Describe the contrast and feeling rather than only looking for bright redness.

When should I stop using a product?

Repeated burning, stinging or tingling are reasons to pause a non-prescription product rather than treating discomfort as proof it is working. Seek professional guidance for severe or persistent symptoms, and ask a prescriber about prescription products.

Can MyMirror analyse a rash or mole?

MyMirror can help organise visible skin patterns for a general skin check, but it does not diagnose rashes, infections or lesions. A changing, bleeding, crusty or unusual mole deserves clinical advice.
