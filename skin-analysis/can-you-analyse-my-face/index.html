Quick answer

What a photo can show—and what it cannot

A clear photo can help you notice visible patterns: where the skin looks shiny, where breakouts cluster, whether old marks are more noticeable than active spots, or whether texture is drawing your eye. It cannot tell you the medical cause, confirm an infection, rule out a skin condition, or prescribe treatment. That boundary is not a limitation to hide; it is what makes a face scan a calmer starting point.

A face map for the concern you notice first

People rarely look in the mirror and see one neatly labelled problem. You may see shine on the nose, a few healing marks on the cheeks and a different texture around the chin—all at once. Mapping the face by area helps turn that blur of concern into a useful observation.

  2. 2. Nose and centre face: observe oil, visible pores and congestion as separate visual cues—not as a verdict about being “dirty” or having bad skin.
  3. 3–4. Cheeks: compare active blemishes with the flat colour left after older spots. These can look similar from a distance but suggest different questions.
  4. 5. Chin and jaw: record timing, tenderness and repetition. A photo can document the pattern; it cannot explain the cause.

Make the image useful before you ask it a big question

A face scan is only as useful as the photo you give it. The American Academy of Dermatology’s photo guidance emphasises clear images that reflect the colour and texture you see in real life. You do not need a perfect selfie; you need a straightforward one.

Use even light

Stand facing a window or another soft, even light source. Strong side light can deepen a shadow below the eye or beside the nose and make it look like a skin change.

Skip filters and beauty mode

Filters can blur texture, alter colour and make the result less honest. The point is to see today’s skin, not improve the image before the scan begins.

Keep the frame simple

Pull hair away if it covers an area you want to review, look forward, and avoid a very close camera angle that can exaggerate pores and texture.

From a visible signal to one sensible next step

The most helpful result is not a long list of flaws. It is a smaller decision: what do I observe, what can I simplify, and when is a professional opinion the better next step?

Your result is not a beauty score

Faces have pores, unevenness, expression lines and changing colour. A useful tool should not turn ordinary human texture into a shopping list. MyMirror is designed to help you name the visible pattern that is bothering you today, then choose a proportionate next step.

Sometimes the right result is simply: do less, change one thing at a time, or take clearer notes before your next dermatology appointment. That is still a useful answer.

The scan does not decide whether a feature needs “fixing.” You decide what you want to understand. If normal texture is the only thing you notice, the most helpful outcome may be reassurance that you do not need to build a new routine around it.

Why “tell me my problems” needs care

When you are frustrated, it is tempting to want a selfie to settle every question at once. But skin concerns can look alike in a photo. The American Academy of Dermatology notes that acne-like concerns may have different explanations, which is why MyMirror does not label a visible pattern as a diagnosis.

Use the scan for organisation and clarity—not medical certainty, reassurance about a worrying lesion, or a reason to try harsher treatments.

A better question than “what is wrong with my face?” is “what is visible, where is it most noticeable, and what would be a sensible next step?” That shift leaves room for your skin to be human and for the next decision to stay small.

When a scan is not the right next step

A photo-led tool is for routine decisions and visible organisation. It is not an emergency service, a diagnostic examination or a replacement for professional medical care.

Choose professional advice if you have:
  • a new or changing mole, lesion or patch that worries you
  • severe pain, swelling, bleeding, pus, fever or a rapidly spreading rash
  • a persistent concern that is affecting your confidence or not improving with careful, simple care
  • a question about diagnosis, prescription treatment or a procedure

When in doubt, the safer answer is to speak with a qualified clinician.

Before you start your scan

  • Use a clear, front-facing image in even light.
  • Remove beauty filters and keep makeup light or absent if possible.
  • Think about what changed: new product, recent breakout, sun exposure, irritation or no obvious trigger.
  • Pick the one visible concern you most want to understand first.

What to do with your result

  • Read it as a visible map, not a diagnosis.
  • Choose one next action rather than replacing your entire routine overnight.
  • Use a relevant MyMirror guide if you need help with acne, marks, oil balance or texture.
  • Take the pattern and timeline to a dermatologist when professional input is the better next move.

Questions people ask before a face analysis

Can MyMirror tell me what skin condition I have?

No. MyMirror helps you organise visible skin patterns and decide what to pay attention to first. It does not diagnose a condition, confirm an infection or replace a qualified dermatologist.

What can the scan look for?

It is designed to help you review visible concerns such as breakouts, oiliness, marks and texture across facial areas. Treat the result as an organised observation, not a medical conclusion.

Do I need an account to start?

No. You can start the MyMirror scan without creating an account.

Will the scan recommend a long list of products?

The most useful outcome is a clearer priority, not product overload. Start with the visible pattern and choose one proportionate next step.

Should I scan with makeup on?

A clear, natural-looking photo is most useful. Heavy makeup, filters, strong shadows and beauty-mode processing can obscure colour and texture.

Can I use the result to track changes?

It can help you organise what you see today. For fair comparisons, take photos in similar light, at a similar distance and without changing many products at once.

What if a spot looks painful, rapidly changing or worrying?

Do not rely on a selfie for that answer. Seek appropriate medical advice, especially for a new or changing mole, lesion, rapidly spreading rash, pain, swelling or bleeding.

Is this a replacement for a dermatologist visit?

No. A scan can help you describe visible patterns and prepare clearer questions, but diagnosis, prescription care and evaluation of worrying changes belong with a qualified clinician.
